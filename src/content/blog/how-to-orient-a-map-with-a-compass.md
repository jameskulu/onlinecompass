At a trail junction, the map shows a path branching to the right. On the ground, two paths seem to fit. Before choosing either, turn the map until its paths point in the same directions as the real ones. That is **orienting a map**, also called setting or orientating it.

An oriented map makes the landscape easier to read: the ridge on your left appears on the left of the map, and the path ahead runs away from your position on the paper. You can do this with a baseplate compass, a phone compass, or identifiable landmarks.

> **Quick method:** Lay the map flat, identify its north reference, and turn the map until that north direction matches the same north reference on your compass. Then check at least two visible features. If the map uses grid north and the compass uses magnetic north, account for the angle between them before accepting the alignment.

## What you need before you start

Use a map with a legible north arrow or labelled north-south grid lines. A baseplate compass is convenient because its straight edge can sit along a grid line. A phone running the [online compass](/compass/) can also give you a heading, provided its sensor is working and you know which north reference it displays.

Choose a place where you can stop safely and hold the map level. Move away from vehicles, steel gates, magnetic phone cases, and other objects that can disturb the compass. If a phone heading jumps while you stand still, work through the [compass calibration guide](/blog/phone-compass-calibration-fix/) before using it to set the map.

You do not have to know your exact position to turn the map north. You **do** need a reliable position before using the oriented map to choose a particular trail or plot a route.

## First, identify which north the map uses

The top edge of the paper is not a reliable north reference on every map. Check the legend, margin, or north arrow.

| North reference | What it means | Where to look |
| --- | --- | --- |
| True north | The direction toward the geographic North Pole | A true-north arrow, sometimes marked with a star |
| Grid north | The northward direction of the map's coordinate grid | Labelled north-south grid lines and a grid-north arrow |
| Magnetic north | The direction indicated by a magnetic compass locally | Magnetic north lines or a magnetic-north arrow |

These directions can differ. The [USGS explanation of map north arrows](https://www.usgs.gov/faqs/what-do-different-north-arrows-a-usgs-topographic-map-mean?items_per_page=6&page=1) distinguishes all three and explains how a topographic map represents them.

For a magnetic compass and a grid map, you need the **grid-to-magnetic angle**. Magnetic declination describes magnetic north relative to **true** north; it is not automatically the same correction. Use the map publisher's information for the relevant location and date. An old printed magnetic-north arrow may no longer represent the current field.

## Orient a map with a baseplate compass

The following method uses an ordinary compass with no preset declination adjustment. It aligns the map directly with magnetic north, avoiding a separate bearing conversion.

1. **Lay the map flat.** Find a north-south grid line and identify which end points toward grid north.
2. **Establish magnetic north on the map.** Using current grid-to-magnetic information, draw a pencil arrow at the correct angle from grid north. If magnetic north is 6° east of grid north, the arrow points 6° clockwise from the northward grid line. If it is 6° west, draw it 6° counterclockwise. Use a protractor or the compass bezel as an angle scale.
3. **Place the compass along that arrow.** Align a long edge of the baseplate with the pencil line, with the direction-of-travel arrow pointing toward its magnetic-north end. Set the bezel to 0° so the orienting arrow points the same way as the baseplate.
4. **Turn the map and compass together.** Keep the compass fixed against the paper. Rotate the whole assembly until the north-seeking end of the needle aligns with the orienting arrow.
5. **Check the ground.** Compare the direction of a nearby path, ridge, or other identifiable feature with its direction on the map.

Natural Resources Canada's [guide to orienting a topographic map](https://prod-natural-resources.azure.cloud.nrcan-rncan.gc.ca/maps-tools-publications/maps/topographic-maps/orienting-topographic-map) describes using a magnetic-north line to align map and compass.

If the map already has suitable, current magnetic-north lines, use one instead of drawing a new line. If your compass has adjustable declination, follow its manufacturer's procedure for the map reference you are using. Avoid applying a preset correction and then applying the same correction again on the paper.

### Worked example: magnetic north is 6° east of grid north

Imagine a map whose current grid-to-magnetic angle is **6° east**. This is an example, not a correction to use for your location.

Draw magnetic north 6° clockwise from a northward grid line. Place the compass along that drawn line and rotate the map until the north-seeking needle points along it.

The needle should now sit **6° clockwise from the map's grid north**, rather than directly over the grid line. That visible gap is expected: it is the correction you deliberately included.

As a numerical check, a direction of 0° grid is 354° magnetic in this example. A path running 90° grid is 84° magnetic. Both describe the same ground directions in different reference systems.

## Orient a paper map with a phone compass

A phone can supply the north direction, but it adds two checks: the displayed reference and the physical edge used for the heading.

1. Open the [online compass](/compass/) and confirm whether the reading uses true or magnetic north.
2. Identify the matching reference on your map. For a true-north display, use the map's true-north arrow. For a magnetic display, use a current magnetic-north line as described above.
3. Hold the phone flat and point its top edge toward north, turning until the heading is near 0°. Keep the screen orientation consistent while doing this.
4. Keeping the phone pointing north, turn the map until the chosen north arrow runs parallel to the phone's top-to-bottom axis and points toward its top edge.
5. Verify the result against visible features before moving.

If you choose true north on the phone, do not substitute a grid line for the map's true-north arrow unless you have accounted for the grid-to-true angle. The [magnetic declination guide](/blog/magnetic-declination-explained/) explains the true-to-magnetic conversion in more detail.

On a digital map, a north-up button can restore the map's display orientation. That alone does not establish which way the phone or your body is facing. Treat the map's orientation and the device's heading as separate information.

## Check the alignment with two landmarks

Once the compass and map agree, test the result against the landscape. This catches a reversed needle, the wrong north reference, and mistaken feature identification.

From a known position, choose two clearly identifiable features in different directions: for example, a bridge and a hilltop. Imagine a line from your position to each feature on the paper. Those lines should point toward the corresponding features on the ground.

A nearby path provides another useful check, but a straight path alone has a 180° ambiguity: its line can fit the map in either direction. A bridge at one end, a bend, or a second landmark resolves that ambiguity.

If both features disagree by a similar angle, recheck the north reference and compass interference. If one fits and the other does not, reconsider the landmark identification or your assumed position. Do not rotate the map until one uncertain feature happens to fit.

## Set the map without a compass

When you know where you are and can identify surrounding features, you can orient the map by matching the ground directly.

At a distinctive path junction, mark your position with a thumb. Turn the map so the path you arrived on points back along the real path. Then check that the other branches, nearby stream, or slope also fall on the correct sides.

This works best with several features whose arrangement is distinctive. A single straight road, a vague hillside, or two similar junctions can produce a convincing but incorrect match. If you cannot identify your position, orienting by a guessed junction compounds the uncertainty. The guide to [finding your position with compass resection](/blog/find-your-location-with-a-compass-resection/) covers a different way to establish a fix using known landmarks.

## What to do after the map is oriented

Keep a thumb on your last confirmed position. Identify the next junction or landmark, then trace the route to it. Check the expected distance and any terrain features that should appear along the way.

Turning your body can turn the paper too. Reorient the map after a stop or change of direction instead of assuming it stayed aligned.

Orienting the map does not make every straight line across it a usable route. A direct line may cross steep ground, water, or private land. Choose a route using the map's terrain and access information. If you need to travel on a measured direction, continue with [how to take and follow a compass bearing](/blog/how-to-take-a-compass-bearing/) and our guide on [how to walk in a straight line with a compass](/blog/how-to-walk-a-straight-line-compass/).

## Common problems and their fixes

| What you notice | What to check |
| --- | --- |
| Everything appears reversed | The north-seeking needle end, and whether your map arrow points north rather than south |
| All features are rotated by a similar angle | Grid, true, and magnetic references; correction direction; compass interference |
| One landmark fits but another does not | Your position and the identity of each landmark |
| The reading changes near a gate or vehicle | Move away and repeat the measurement |
| The map fits until you turn to talk or walk | Reorient the paper after turning your body |
| The map's top edge disagrees with its north arrow | Use the labelled arrow or grid reference rather than the page edge |

## Frequently asked questions

### Do I need to orient the map before measuring a bearing on it?

No. Measuring a grid bearing is a geometry task: align the baseplate with your route and the bezel's orienting lines with the northward grid. The map can lie in any direction during that measurement. Orienting it helps you compare the route with the ground. You still need the appropriate north correction before following the bearing with a magnetic compass.

### Should the red needle line up with the map's vertical grid lines?

Only when magnetic north and grid north coincide, or when the difference is small enough for the particular task. Otherwise, an accurately oriented map leaves the needle at the local grid-to-magnetic angle to those lines.

### Can I orient a map if I do not know where I am?

Yes, using a reliable compass and the correct north reference. That establishes the map's direction, not your position. Find a trustworthy position before deciding which mapped junction or path is yours.

### Does pointing the map north tell me which way to walk?

It lets you relate mapped directions to the landscape. To choose where to walk, you also need your position, your destination, and a suitable route between them. Practice those steps at a familiar park or junction, where you can check the answer immediately.
