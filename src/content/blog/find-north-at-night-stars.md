A compass is a convenience until the battery dies, the needle sticks, or a steel bridge warps the reading. The night sky has no such weaknesses. Every star you can see is wheeling around a single point in the sky, and that point sits above **true north** — or true south, if you are in the southern hemisphere. Find the pivot, and you have found a direction no magnet can argue with.

This guide covers the whole skill: how to locate Polaris quickly, what to do when the Big Dipper is hidden, how to find south with the Southern Cross, how to read a star-trail photo, what the Moon can and cannot tell you, and how to turn a patch of sky into a bearing you can walk on. The [live compass](/compass/) and the [sun & moon tool](/sun-moon/) are useful cross-checks; the sky is the fallback that never runs out of power.

> **In short:** In the northern hemisphere, find the two outer stars of the Big Dipper's bowl, run a line through them, and extend it about five times their separation to reach **Polaris**. Polaris sits within about two-thirds of a degree of true north, so the direction from you to Polaris *is* north. In the southern hemisphere, extend the long axis of the **Southern Cross** about 4.5 times its length to reach the south celestial pole, and drop straight down to the horizon for south. Stars give **true** north; convert to magnetic before you use a compass bearing.

## Why the night sky points north

The Earth turns once every 23 hours 56 minutes, so the stars appear to rotate around the celestial poles at about **15° per hour**. Everything in the sky shares that motion — the Dipper, Orion, the Southern Cross, all of it. The two points that do not move are the north and south celestial poles, and they sit directly above true north and true south.

That single fact is the whole method. You are not memorising a star map. You are locating a fixed point, and the constellations are just signposts that happen to be reliable for the next few thousand years.

There is a second useful fact: **the altitude of the celestial pole above your horizon equals your latitude.** At 51°N, Polaris is 51° above the northern horizon. At the equator it sits on the horizon; at the North Pole it is overhead. If you can estimate angles with your hand — a fist at arm's length is roughly 10°, a spread hand about 20° — you can sanity-check your latitude and your Polaris sighting at the same time.

## Finding Polaris, the North Star

Polaris is the most famous star in the sky and almost everyone is surprised by it the first time: **it is not bright.** At around second magnitude it is roughly the 48th brightest star, dimmer than the stars of the Big Dipper that point to it. It is famous for its position, not its brilliance.

It marks the end of the handle of the **Little Dipper** (Ursa Minor), a faint, compact shape that is genuinely hard to pick out from a suburban garden. Do not start there. Start with the Big Dipper.

### Method 1: the Big Dipper pointer stars

The Big Dipper is part of Ursa Major and is recognisable almost everywhere north of the tropics. Its bowl has four stars; the two at the **outer edge of the bowl**, away from the handle, are **Merak** and **Dubhe**. They are the pointers.

1. Find the Big Dipper. Seven bright stars, four forming a ladle bowl and three a handle.
2. Identify the two stars at the front of the bowl — the edge furthest from the handle.
3. Draw an imaginary line from the lower, inner pointer (Merak) through the upper, outer one (Dubhe).
4. Extend that line about **five times** the distance between the two stars.
5. The line lands on Polaris.

A handy memory aid: the line leaves the bowl *over the top*, in the direction the bowl is "pouring". If you extend it too far you will pass Polaris and find nothing bright, because there is nothing bright there — the emptiness is part of the signature.

### Method 2: Cassiopeia, the backup signpost

When the Dipper is low, partly set, or hidden behind a hill, use **Cassiopeia**. It is a bright, unmistakable **W** (or M, depending on the season) on the opposite side of Polaris from the Dipper.

The middle star of the W is roughly on the line between the Dipper and Polaris. Better still: the centre of the W sits about **30° from Polaris**, almost exactly opposite the Dipper. Draw a line from the Dipper's bowl through Polaris and keep going; you arrive at Cassiopeia. Two signposts on opposite sides of the pole is a built-in error check — if the geometry does not work, you have misidentified one of them.

### Method 3: let the camera do it

If you have a phone with a pro or night mode, point it at the northern sky, rest it on something solid, and take the longest exposure it will allow — 10 to 30 seconds, or several stacked. The stars become short arcs. Extend the arcs into circles, and **the centre of the circles is the celestial pole**.

This is the most elegant method because it needs no constellation knowledge at all, and it works in the southern hemisphere too. A 15-minute exposure gives unmistakable concentric trails; a 30-second one gives short strokes whose curvature still points to the centre.

### Method 4: the drift test

If you are unsure which faint star is Polaris, watch the candidate for two or three minutes. Polaris barely moves. The stars around it crawl anticlockwise in the northern sky, so a star that drifts steadily relative to a fixed reference — a roof edge, a branch — is not Polaris. This is slow, but it settles the question when the Dipper is marginal.

## The southern hemisphere: the Southern Cross and the Pointers

There is no bright southern pole star. **Sigma Octantis** sits near the south celestial pole but at magnitude 5.5 it is barely visible to the naked eye, and hopeless in any light pollution. Instead, the southern sky gives you two independent constructions, and you use their intersection.

### The long-axis rule

**Crux**, the Southern Cross, is a compact kite of four bright stars plus a fifth. Its **long axis** runs from Gacrux at the top through Acrux at the foot — Acrux is the brightest and usually the lowest of the four. Extend that axis, from top through foot, about **4.5 times the length of the cross**, and you arrive near the south celestial pole.

That is an approximation. It is good to roughly 5°, which is fine for walking and useless for surveying.

### The Pointers, for a cross-check

Nearby are the two brightest stars of Centaurus, **Alpha and Beta Centauri**, known as the Pointers. They sit roughly in line with the cross's long axis and point toward it. Construct the **perpendicular bisector** of the line joining the two Pointers, and extend it until it crosses the extended long axis of the Cross. The intersection is the south celestial pole, and it is more reliable than the 4.5× rule on its own.

From that point, drop vertically to the horizon. Where the line meets the horizon is **due south**.

### Telling Crux from the False Cross

The southern sky contains an impostor: the **False Cross**, made of stars in Vela and Carina. It is larger, looser, and dimmer than the real thing, and its long axis points nowhere useful.

Two checks separate them. First, the real Crux is compact and bright, with a distinctive fifth star inside the kite. Second, the real Crux has the two brilliant **Pointers** nearby, one of them the third-brightest star in the entire night sky. The False Cross has no such escort.

Crux is circumpolar south of about 30°S, fully visible from most of the southern hemisphere, and invisible from latitudes north of roughly 30°N. If you cannot see it, you are probably not far enough south — or it is the wrong season and the Cross is below the horizon at that hour.

## East and west from Orion's belt

North and south are the hard directions; east and west are comparatively easy, because the whole sky rises in the east and sets in the west.

The cleanest tool is **Orion**, whose three belt stars lie almost exactly on the celestial equator. Because of that, Orion's belt rises **almost due east** and sets **almost due west** — within a degree or two, far better than most people expect.

- If Orion is climbing out of the eastern horizon, you are looking east.
- If it is sinking into the west, you are looking west.
- If it is due south (northern hemisphere) at its highest, it is around local midnight in December and January.

Orion is an evening object from roughly November to March and a pre-dawn object in the late summer and autumn. When it is not available, the zodiac constellations — Leo, Virgo, Scorpius, Sagittarius — follow the same rule with slightly more error, because they sit near the ecliptic rather than exactly on the equator.

One caution: a constellation's rising point drifts through the year for exactly the same reason the Sun's does. Only stars on the celestial equator rise due east. Use Orion as a compass, not as a protractor.

## Using the Moon (carefully)

The Moon is the least precise night-time compass, but it is often the only object visible through cloud, and its geometry is simple: **the illuminated limb always faces the Sun, and the horns point away from it.**

So a line drawn perpendicular to the line joining the two horns, toward the bright side, points at the Sun's position below the horizon. If you know the time, that tells you roughly where the Sun is — and therefore roughly where west or east lies, since the Sun sets in the west and rises in the east.

The phase also works as a crude clock, which is often more useful than the direction:

| Phase | Rises | Highest | Sets | Use |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| New | With the Sun | Noon | With the Sun | Useless — no Moon |
| Waxing crescent | Mid-morning | Afternoon | Early evening | Low in the west after sunset |
| First quarter | Around noon | Sunset | Around midnight | Due south at dusk (north hemisphere) |
| Full | Sunset | Midnight | Sunrise | Rises in the east as the Sun sets |
| Last quarter | Around midnight | Sunrise | Around noon | Due south at dawn (north hemisphere) |
| Waning crescent | After midnight | Pre-dawn | Afternoon | Low in the east before sunrise |

Two warnings. The Moon's orbit is tilted about 5° to the ecliptic and its declination swings through roughly ±28.5°, so its rising and setting points wander far more than the Sun's. And the lit side appears to switch sides between hemispheres — waxing looks lit on the right from the northern hemisphere and on the left from the southern — which trips up half of everyone who tries to use it. Treat the Moon as a **10–20° sanity check**, never as your primary bearing.

## From the sky to a bearing you can walk

Everything above gives you **true** north. Your compass needle gives you **magnetic** north. The difference is magnetic declination, and it is the step where most night navigators quietly go wrong.

The rule, stated once:

```
true bearing      = magnetic bearing + east declination
magnetic bearing  = true bearing     − east declination
```

For a west declination, swap the signs: subtract to go true, add to go magnetic. The [full declination guide](/blog/magnetic-declination-explained/) has the regional values and the map diagram; the short version is that you must know your local declination before you compare a star bearing to a compass.

| Declination | Magnetic bearing | True bearing |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 12° E | 040° | 052° |
| 12° E | 288° (true) | 276° (magnetic) |
| 8° W | 100° | 092° |
| 8° W | 045° (true) | 053° (magnetic) |
| 0° | Same | Same |

The practical sequence at night:

1. **Face Polaris** (or the point above the south celestial pole) and hold that direction.
2. **Pick a landmark on that line** — a tree, a gap in a ridge, a lit window. A bearing without a landmark is a number you will lose in thirty seconds.
3. **Read your compass** while facing that landmark. If your compass is set to true north, it should read 000°. If it reads 000° and the stars disagree, your compass is not set to true north — a classic and expensive mistake.
4. **Write the number down** and record whether it is true or magnetic. Then convert once, deliberately, before you walk.
5. **Re-check every ten or fifteen minutes.** A bearing is a snapshot; your position changes and so does the angle to your landmark.

If you are navigating to a specific point rather than just "north", the [bearing calculator](/bearing/) turns a start and finish coordinate into an exact initial bearing and distance, and the [location tool](/location/) gives you the coordinates to feed it. The stars tell you which way is which; the arithmetic tells you which way to go.

## How accurate is star navigation?

The honest answer depends entirely on the method, and the differences are large enough to plan around.

| Method | Typical error | Best for | Main weakness |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Polaris direct | ~1° | Anything, any clear night | Needs an unobstructed northern horizon |
| Big Dipper pointers | 2–3° | Fast fixes at mid-northern latitudes | Dipper low or hidden in some seasons |
| Cassiopeia | 3–5° | When the Dipper is unavailable | Fainter, more spread out |
| Star-trail photo | 1–2° | Cloud gaps, unfamiliar skies, teaching | Needs a steady rest and a long exposure |
| Southern Cross + Pointers | 3–5° | Southern hemisphere route-finding | No bright pole star to confirm against |
| Southern Cross, 4.5× rule alone | up to 10° | Rough orientation | Sensitive to misjudging the axis |
| Orion's belt, rising/setting | ~5° | East–west checks | Seasonal, only near the horizon |
| The Moon | 10–20° | Last resort under cloud | Phase geometry, declination swing |

Convert those angles into ground error and the stakes become obvious. A 5° bearing error puts you **87 m off course after one kilometre**, and 875 m off after ten. That is the same arithmetic as any compass work — which is why the [bearing guide](/blog/how-to-take-a-compass-bearing/) matters just as much at night as in daylight.

| Bearing error | 500 m | 1 km | 5 km |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1° | 9 m | 17 m | 87 m |
| 3° | 26 m | 52 m | 262 m |
| 5° | 44 m | 87 m | 437 m |
| 10° | 88 m | 176 m | 875 m |

## Nine mistakes that ruin a night fix

1. **Looking for a bright North Star.** Polaris is modest. Find the pointers first, then confirm the faint star they land on.
2. **Confusing Polaris with the rest of the Little Dipper.** The whole asterism is faint; only the end of the handle is the pole star.
3. **Using a star close to the horizon.** Atmospheric refraction lifts objects within 10° of the horizon by up to about 5°, and haze makes it worse. Sight stars at least 20–30° up.
4. **Skipping declination.** True north from the stars and magnetic north from the needle are not the same direction, and in some places they differ by 20° or more.
5. **Mistaking the False Cross for Crux.** Check for the two Pointers and the compact kite shape.
6. **Assuming Polaris is exactly on the pole.** It is about 0.65° off. Irrelevant for walking, relevant for surveying, and worth knowing before you blame the compass.
7. **Wrecking your night vision.** A white phone screen costs you 20–30 minutes of dark adaptation. Use a red filter or the dimmest setting, and give your eyes ten minutes before you judge the sky.
8. **Calling a planet a star.** Venus, Jupiter, Mars, and Saturn are bright, steady, and sit near the ecliptic. A brilliant "star" that does not fit the pattern is usually a planet — and planets wander, so they make poor signposts.
9. **Trusting one sighting.** Confirm with a second method — Polaris plus Cassiopeia, or the Cross plus the Pointers. Agreement between two independent constructions is worth more than a confident single glance.

## A ten-minute field drill

This works in a garden, a park, or a campsite, and it is the fastest way to make the skill permanent.

1. On a clear night, find Polaris with the Big Dipper and hold the direction.
2. Without moving, confirm it with Cassiopeia on the opposite side of the pole.
3. Take a compass reading toward Polaris with the compass set to true north. It should be 000° ± 2°.
4. Switch the compass to magnetic north and note the difference. That is your local declination — check it against a declination map.
5. Photograph the northern sky for 30 seconds on a steady rest and confirm the arcs circle the same point.
6. Repeat on a different night, an hour earlier or later. The sky has rotated; the pole has not.

Do that once and you will never again be lost at night with a working phone and no signal. Do it in the southern hemisphere with the Cross and the Pointers, and you have the other half of the world covered.

## Night navigation, safely

A bearing is a direction, not a guarantee. At night, the ground hides itself: cliffs, bog, drainage ditches, and fence wire all disappear a metre from your feet.

- **Navigate to a handrail**, not a bearing. A stream, a ridge, a track, or a wall that runs the right way is worth more than a perfect angle.
- **Count your pace** on the leg you can see, and stop at the end of it. Do not walk a bearing blindly across unknown ground.
- **Use a red headtorch** and keep it low. Bright white light destroys night vision — yours and your companions'.
- **Set a turn-around time** before you leave, and honour it. Being benighted is a planning failure, not a navigation one.
- **Tell someone your route** and expected return. This is the single highest-value habit in all of outdoor navigation.
- **Keep the phone in reserve.** Use the [online compass](/compass/) and the [sun & moon tool](/sun-moon/) while the battery lasts, and treat the sky as the backup that always works. The [calibration guide](/blog/phone-compass-calibration-fix/) explains why a phone reading can drift, and the [sun guide](/blog/find-direction-using-the-sun/) covers the daylight version of the same skill.

## Frequently asked questions

**Is Polaris always exactly north?** Almost. It circles the north celestial pole once a day at a radius of about 0.65°, so the direction to it is true north to within a degree. That is more than accurate enough for walking, and not accurate enough for surveying.

**What is the brightest star in the sky?** Sirius, not Polaris. Polaris is roughly second magnitude and easy to miss if you are expecting a beacon. Use the Big Dipper's pointer stars rather than scanning for brightness.

**How do I find north at night if I can't see the Big Dipper?** Use Cassiopeia's W, or take a long-exposure photo and find the centre of the star trails. Both work without any constellation knowledge beyond recognising one shape.

**Does the North Star work in the southern hemisphere?** No. Polaris is below the horizon for most of the southern hemisphere. Use the Southern Cross with the Pointers, or a star-trail photo centred on the south celestial pole.

**What is the Southern Cross method, in one sentence?** Extend the long axis of Crux about 4.5 times its length, cross it with the perpendicular bisector of the two Pointers, and drop from the intersection to the horizon for due south.

**Can the Moon tell me which way is north?** Only roughly. Its lit side faces the Sun and its horns point away from it, so it hints at the Sun's direction and the time. Expect 10–20° of error and never rely on it alone.

**Why does my compass disagree with the stars?** Either your compass is set to magnetic north and the stars are giving you true north — that is declination, and it is expected — or something nearby is distorting the magnetometer. Check the declination value first, then step away from metal and calibrate.

**Is it true that you can find north with a watch at night?** No. The watch method depends on the Sun. At night there is no Sun to point the hour hand at, and the Moon moves too fast to substitute.

**Can I use a stargazing app instead?** Yes, and it is excellent for identifying what you are looking at. But an app is a map, not a skill: it needs a battery, a screen, and usually a network. Learn the two or three star patterns here and you will not need it.

**How long should a star-trail exposure be?** Fifteen minutes gives clean concentric arcs; thirty seconds gives short strokes that still reveal the centre. Use a tripod, a wall, or a rock, and switch off image stabilisation.

## The bottom line

The night sky is the most reliable direction-finder you own, and it costs nothing. Find the pivot, and you have true north — or true south — with no magnet, no battery, and no signal.

Learn **one** method properly: the Big Dipper pointers in the north, or the Southern Cross and Pointers in the south. Add the star-trail photo as a universal backup. Then do the one thing that turns knowledge into navigation — take the bearing, apply your declination, pick a landmark on the line, and walk to it. When the stars, the compass, and the map all agree, you can move with confidence; when they disagree, you now know exactly which one to check first.

If you want to close the loop, read the [declination guide](/blog/magnetic-declination-explained/) and the [bearing guide](/blog/how-to-take-a-compass-bearing/), then practise the daylight versions with the [online compass](/compass/) and — when the terrain is confusing — the [resection method](/blog/find-your-location-with-a-compass-resection/). Night navigation is the same skill with the lights off, and it is worth having before you need it.
